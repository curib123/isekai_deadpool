# Chapter 004 - Red Jackal Tells You: The Summoning Error: A Question with Teeth

The golden light swallowed us.

Not metaphorically. It made a noise like a giant mouth closing around a handful of stars, and then it pulled my stomach, my hair, and several private opinions through the doorway.

The maintenance creature made a happy clicking sound.

The woman did not.

“Is this supposed to happen?” I asked.

“Stop talking.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is an instruction.”

“Then you should make it more interesting.”

The light vanished.

I landed on my face.

My sword landed beside me.

The maintenance creature landed on top of the sword, looked at me with six eyes, and sat down as if it had paid for the seat.

The woman landed on both feet.

I disliked her immediately.

The first thing I noticed was the smell of wet stone. The second was the rain. The third was the sky, which had the good sense to be gray and miserable instead of cheerful.

We were standing inside a broken stone building. Half the roof was missing. Tall pillars leaned around us, each one carved with symbols that had been worn smooth by weather and time. Grass grew through the cracks in the floor. A dead vine hung from one wall like the building had tried to strangle itself and lost interest halfway through.

Beyond the open archway, mountains rose beneath the clouds.

I sat up.

“This is the mortal world.”

The woman looked around. “Yes.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“The destination is correct.”

“The entrance is not.”

“The entrance is acceptable.”

I looked at the roof. A chunk of stone fell from the top of a pillar and broke beside my shoe.

“Your definition of acceptable needs medical attention.”

The woman stepped toward the doorway and raised one hand. The air above her palm became a small map made of light. It showed a mountain, a road, a river, and several red marks that probably meant danger or excellent restaurants.

“We are outside the intended transfer point,” she said.

“How far outside?”

“Three valleys.”

“That sounds like a lot of outside.”

“The summoning coordinates shifted.”

“Because of me?”

“Because of the error.”

“You keep calling it an error like that makes it less personal.”

“It is not personal.”

“You summoned a man with no name, no record, no class, no level, no skills, and a maintenance animal that followed us through a forbidden door.”

The creature clicked proudly.

“That is not an error,” I continued. “That is a group project.”

The woman turned toward me.

“You were not the intended arrival.”

“Who was?”

She did not answer.

I stood.

The movement should have hurt. It did not. My body had already repaired itself, which was convenient and deeply suspicious. I touched my ribs anyway, because sometimes you needed to check whether the universe had quietly changed its return policy.

“You are avoiding the question.”

“The question is not relevant.”

“That is what people say when the question is relevant enough to ruin their afternoon.”

“You need to understand your situation.”

“I understand several parts. I am dead. I am alive. I am in a fantasy world. The local paperwork hates me. You are either a goddess or a government employee with excellent fabric. There is a monster wearing a badge. And you summoned somebody else.”

The woman stared at me.

“That was a good summary,” I said.

“You are impossible.”

“You keep saying that like it is not the best thing about me.”

Rain struck the broken roof. The maintenance creature climbed onto my shoulder and settled there with the confidence of a scarf that had recently acquired opinions.

“It likes you,” the woman said.

“It has taste.”

“It is a corridor warden. It should not be here.”

“Then it is also an illegal immigrant. We have something in common.”

“Do not call it that.”

“Fine. It is an unauthorized workplace transfer.”

The creature licked my ear.

“And it is gross.”

The woman looked toward the mountains.

“We need to reach the nearest settlement before night.”

“Why?”

“Because this place is dangerous.”

“I have regeneration.”

“That does not make you safe.”

“It makes me difficult to kill.”

“Those are different things.”

I looked at her.

That answer had teeth.

Not the cute maintenance kind. The other kind. The kind that said she had seen people survive wounds and still lose everything around them.

I looked away first.

You may have noticed that I did not make a joke.

Do not get excited. It was not character growth. The moment simply deserved better manners.

The woman lowered the glowing map.

“You need food, clothing, shelter, and a legal identity.”

“I have a name.”

“You have an answer you repeat when questioned.”

“That is what a name is.”

“Not here.”

“Then your world is doing names wrong.”

“Your name has to exist in the World Registry.”

“What if I choose a new one?”

“It will still be undefined.”

“Then I can save money on paperwork.”

“You need a registration.”

“I have never met a bureaucracy I could not disappoint.”

“You are going to meet several.”

“Wonderful. I was worried the afterlife would be relaxing.”

She looked at the sword beside my feet.

“Where did that come from?”

“I imagined it.”

“The System did not grant it.”

“The System has not granted me anything.”

“You cannot simply create whatever you want.”

I lifted the sword.

“I just did.”

“That was a coincidence.”

I imagined a red umbrella.

An umbrella appeared in my hand.

It was enormous. It had a silver handle shaped like a question mark and a printed message across the fabric:

**ASK BETTER QUESTIONS.**

The woman stared at it.

I opened the umbrella above her head.

The rain stopped touching her.

“That is not a coincidence,” I said.

“It is a violation.”

“That is a stronger word. I like it.”

“The world has rules.”

“The world has suggestions. I have noticed the difference.”

The woman reached for the umbrella.

I imagined it becoming smaller.

It became a spoon.

She caught the spoon.

“You changed it.”

“You were going to take it.”

“It is an umbrella.”

“It was an umbrella.”

She stared at the spoon, then at me.

“You are doing this deliberately.”

“I do almost everything deliberately.”

The creature on my shoulder clicked.

“Except bringing that thing,” she said.

“That was a team decision.”

The mountain wind moved through the broken building. Far below, something howled. Another sound answered from the opposite valley.

The woman closed her hand around the spoon.

“We move now.”

“I have questions.”

“Ask while walking.”

“What is this world called?”

“That is a question.”

“Thank you.”

“The world has several names.”

“Pick the one printed on the welcome sign.”

“There is no welcome sign.”

“Then customer service is worse than I thought.”

She walked toward the doorway.

I followed, because the alternative was remaining inside a roofless ruin while the mountain sounds grew closer. I prefer freedom, but I also prefer not being eaten before breakfast.

Outside, the ground sloped toward a narrow path between black trees. Their leaves were dark green and shaped like knives. A blue bird watched us from a branch, then turned its head too far around to be considered healthy.

I pointed at it.

“That bird is judging me.”

“It is a night watcher.”

“It is judging me professionally.”

The woman kept walking.

“You will need to learn the rules of this world.”

“Can I rewrite them?”

“No.”

I waited.

She stopped.

“What?”

“You were supposed to say something more dramatic.”

“No.”

“You could have said, ‘Not without paying the price.’”

“No.”

“Or, ‘Every rule has a consequence.’”

“No.”

“You are terrible at exposition.”

“And you are terrible at listening.”

“That is why we balance each other.”

The path descended into the forest.

For the first time since waking in the white chamber, I saw a road marker. It was a tall stone with three arrows carved into it. One pointed toward the mountains. One pointed toward the valley. The third pointed straight into a wall of thorn bushes.

I touched the marker.

The stone spoke.

“Choose your destination.”

I looked at the woman.

“Does everything here talk?”

“Only enchanted markers.”

“That is a disappointing distinction.”

The marker spoke again.

“Choose your destination.”

“Do you have options?” I asked.

“Mountain. Valley. Thorn.”

“What is in the thorn?”

“Thorn.”

“Helpful.”

I looked at you.

“This was the first moment I understood that the world had a sense of humor. It was not a good sense of humor, but neither was mine when I was tired.”

The woman pointed to the valley.

“The settlement is that way.”

“Then why did the marker offer the thorn?”

“Because some travelers need to be punished.”

“For what?”

“Poor judgment.”

I placed my hand on the thorn arrow.

The woman grabbed my wrist.

“No.”

“I was only looking.”

“You were thinking.”

“Thinking is not illegal.”

“With you, it is dangerous.”

I smiled.

She did not.

The marker repeated its options.

“Mountain. Valley. Thorn.”

I chose the valley.

Not because it was safe.

Because it was the only choice that had not insulted me yet.

The woman walked beside me as the path descended. The maintenance creature rode my shoulder. The stone marker faded behind us.

Ahead, through the trees, a thin line of smoke rose into the gray sky.

Civilization.

People.

Paperwork.

Possibly food.

I looked at the reader.

“If the first town has a guild, I am going to join it.”

The woman frowned.

“You do not know what a guild is here.”

“I know enough.”

“You know nothing.”

“That has never stopped anyone important.”

She opened her mouth to argue.

I changed the sentence before she could.

“We will reach the settlement before sunset,” she said.

Much better.

The smoke ahead thickened.

Something screamed in the forest behind us.

The woman drew a blade of light.

I raised my red sword.

The maintenance creature bared its teeth.

I looked toward the smoke and grinned.

“All right,” I told you. “Let us meet the locals.”
