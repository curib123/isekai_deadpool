# Chapter 001 - Red Jackal Tells You: Before the Isekai - Did the Deadpool Fan Really Die?

I died on a Tuesday.

Yes, that is the opening line. No, you do not get a refund.

Not a Friday. Not during a thunderstorm with lightning striking behind me. Not while a beautiful woman cried over my broken body and promised that she would never love again.

Tuesday.

You would be surprised how much dignity a weekday removes from a death scene.

Before you ask, yes, I know how that sounds. I am telling you my own story, and I have decided to begin with the part where I die. It is not the most original opening, but originality is difficult when your life ends before the plot properly introduces you. Also, starting with my childhood would require remembering my childhood, and memory is currently being managed by a committee of frightened pigeons.

Actually, that sentence is too dramatic.

I crossed it out.

My life did not end before the plot introduced me. The plot had introduced me several times. I had simply ignored the introductions because most of them involved bills, bad sleep, and people asking when I planned to find a more respectable hobby.

There. Better.

The rain began while I was leaving a comic shop that stayed open far too late for a business selling paper, plastic, and the emotional damage of collectible prices. I stood beneath the awning with a red-and-black hoodie pulled over my head and a small box tucked under my arm.

The box contained a Deadpool figure.

It was not the expensive one. The expensive one had a sword, two guns, three interchangeable faces, and a price tag that suggested it had been personally blessed by a minor god. Mine had one sword, one gun, one face, and a left hand that looked slightly confused about its purpose. Honestly, relatable.

I loved it immediately.

The shop clerk leaned against the doorway and watched me protect the box from the rain.

“You bought another one?” she asked.

“I bought a different one.”

“It is the same character.”

“That is how characters work.”

“You bought the same comic three times last month.”

“Different covers.”

“The covers were almost identical.”

“Almost is doing a lot of work in that sentence.”

She looked at the box. “Does this one at least have both hands?”

I checked the picture. “Probably.”

“That is not a confident answer.”

“I appreciate your concern, but I have made peace with manufacturing defects.”

She laughed, which was good. People should laugh when I make a joke. It made the world feel organized.

The clerk pointed at the street. “You walking home in that?”

“I have a jacket.”

“You are wearing a hoodie.”

“A hoodie is a jacket with better public relations.”

“It is raining sideways.”

“Then I will walk diagonally.”

She stared at me for a second.

“You know,” she said, “most people just wait for the rain to stop.”

“Most people also have the survival instincts of background characters.”

“And you?”

I held up the box. “I have a mission.”

The clerk shook her head, but she was still smiling when I stepped into the rain.

"Your mission is to buy plastic?" she called after me.

"My mission is to preserve culture," I called back.

"You said culture could not be eaten."

"That was before I became responsible for it."

That was the last normal conversation I ever had.

I am aware that calling it normal is unfair. The conversation included a discussion about defective action figures, diagonal walking, and background characters. Still, compared to what came afterward, it was practically a family dinner.

The rain blurred the streetlights into long yellow lines. Cars moved slowly through the wet road. Somewhere behind me, the shop's metal shutter began to roll down with the grinding sound of a machine that had given up on happiness.

My phone vibrated.

“You left already?” my friend asked when I answered.

“I am outside.”

“In the rain?”

“No. I am standing inside an extremely realistic special effect. The production value is terrible.”

“You went to the comic shop again.”

“I went to the comic shop for the first time today.”

“That was not the question.”

“Then ask better questions. I am not responsible for the quality of your interview.”

There was a pause. I could hear traffic through the phone on his side and rain through the world on mine.

“Are you bringing food?” he asked.

“I brought culture.”

“Can culture be eaten?”

“Not with that attitude. Food is very sensitive to judgment.”

“You promised dinner.”

“I promised to consider dinner.”

“You said, and I quote, ‘I will return with food like a victorious warrior.’”

“That does sound like me. I was probably wearing a cape in the memory.”

“You are impossible.”

“Technically, I am very possible. I am currently walking, breathing, and making several decisions you will later describe as preventable.”

“Red—”

He stopped himself before using my name. I noticed it because I always noticed when people hesitated around me. They thought I was not listening. I was usually listening. I simply preferred to make them regret speaking first.

“What?” I asked.

“Just get home.”

“That is a boring ending. I refuse to die before the second act.”

“It is a safe ending.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

“For once, let them be the same thing.”

I smiled.

“I will call you when I get home.”

“You better.”

The call ended.

I should have listened to him.

I should have turned around. I should have waited under the shop awning. I should have walked to the crossing instead of taking the shorter route through the narrow street.

I could also have changed the scene.

I am not telling you this to sound impressive. If I wanted to sound impressive, I would have worn a coat and described the rain as symbolic.

That is the part people usually dislike when I tell them this story. They want an accident to be an accident. They want death to be stronger than the person standing in front of it.

I could have imagined the rain stopping.

I could have imagined the road becoming dry.

I could have imagined the van losing its engine, its wheels, or its entire concept of forward motion.

I could have imagined myself somewhere else.

I could have rewritten the next paragraph so the van never entered the street.

I knew I could do all of that.

I still stepped forward.

Future historians may call this bravery.

Future historians will not be consulted.

The van appeared at the end of the street with its headlights cutting through the rain. It was moving too fast for the road. The tires slid. The driver shouted something I could not hear through the storm.

A little boy ran after a red ball.

His mother screamed.

The boy stopped in the middle of the road.

Everything became very clear.

I looked at the boy. I looked at the van. Then I looked at you.

Yes, you.

Do not pretend you were not watching.

I had enough time to solve the problem in a hundred easy ways. I could have placed the boy on the sidewalk. I could have turned the van into a harmless paper model. I could have given myself wings, armor, teleportation, or the physical durability of a joke that refuses to die.

Instead, I imagined a red wall between the boy and the van.

The wall appeared.

"You are welcome," I told the reader.

The boy did not hear me. The reader did. This is an important distinction, mostly because one of us has the decency to appreciate good timing.

Not a normal wall. A huge red cartoon wall with white cracks painted across it and a black sign that said **NOPE** in letters large enough to insult the entire alphabet.

The van hit it.

The wall folded around the front of the vehicle like soft rubber. The boy flew backward into his mother's arms. The driver was thrown sideways but landed on a stack of trash bags that burst beneath him with a sound I would have appreciated under different circumstances.

Nobody died.

Except me.

The wall had protected everyone in front of me. It had not protected me because I had not asked it to.

The van spun. The back end struck the wall, bounced away, and came straight toward me.

I could still change it.

I could still make the van disappear.

I could still make the entire street forget that anything had happened.

I did not.

Yes, I know. You are disappointed.

Please place all complaints in the nearest suggestion box. The nearest suggestion box is also me.

“Why?” you ask.

That is a fair question.

I wish I had a better answer.

Maybe I wanted to know what death felt like. Maybe I was tired of pretending that every problem needed my hand on the page. Maybe some part of me believed I would simply wake up somewhere else, because that was how stories worked.

Or maybe I was an idiot holding a defective action figure in the rain.

The van hit me.

Pain arrived before the sound. My ribs broke. My shoulder went somewhere shoulders were not supposed to go. The comic figure flew from my hand and landed face-first in a puddle.

I remember thinking that the figure had finally found a more dramatic death than the character it represented.

Then the ground struck me.

The world turned white.

“Stay with me!” someone shouted.

“I am trying,” I said.

“Don’t move!”

“That advice arrived late.”

“Can you hear me?”

“Unfortunately. You have a very loud voice.”

Hands pressed against my chest. Rain touched my face. The red-and-black figure sat beside me in the road, one plastic arm pointing toward the sky.

“What is your name?” the paramedic asked.

I knew the answer.

I knew several answers.

My real name was somewhere behind a locked door. I could feel the door. I could not remember what was written on it.

The name that remained clear rose to my mouth first.

“Deadpool.”

The paramedic looked at the other responder.

“No,” he said. “Your name.”

“That is my name.”

“No, it isn't.”

“You know him?”

“Who?”

“Deadpool.”

The paramedic pressed harder on my chest. “Stay awake.”

“That did not answer my question.”

The siren grew louder. The rain grew colder. My heart made one last attempt to become a dramatic sound effect.

I could have rewritten the ending.

I did not.

Not because I was trapped.

Because I wanted to know whether the next scene had better writing.

The world stopped.

Then I opened my eyes in front of a woman wearing enough white fabric and gold jewelry to qualify as either a goddess or someone with terrible financial priorities.

She smiled at me.

“Welcome, lost soul.”

I crossed out her sentence.

“Welcome, valued customer,” she said instead.

Her smile froze.

I smiled back.

"Relax," I said. "If I wanted to make the greeting worse, you would already be wearing a hat."

She looked at her head.

There was no hat.

That was when she understood the problem.

That was how I learned two things.

First, death was not the end.

Second, the universe had terrible customer service.

The woman looked at the air beside my shoulder. A transparent window appeared there, filled with lines of text.

**Name:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Race:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Class:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Level:** UNDEFINED<br>
**HP:** UNDEFINED<br>
**MP:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Attributes:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Skills:** UNDEFINED<br>
**Progression:** UNAVAILABLE<br>
**World Registry:** NO RECORD<br>
**Soul Registry:** NO RECORD<br>
**System Recognition:** FAILED<br>
**Classification:** ANOMALY

I read the list twice.

“That seems rude,” I said. “You could have at least called me handsome before declaring me undefined.”

The woman did not laugh.

“What are you?” she asked.

I looked at the status window. I looked at the empty white room. I looked at the reader who had followed me all the way here.

Then I gave the only answer that made sense.

“Deadpool.”

The woman closed her eyes.

“No.”

“You know him?”

“I do not know what that means.”

I looked at you.

This was your fault. You brought me to a fantasy world with no cultural education program.

I sat up. My broken ribs were gone. My shoulder had returned to its proper location. Somewhere behind me, my memories were still missing.

But I remembered Deadpool.

I remembered the jokes.

I remembered the violence.

I remembered the impossible survival.

I remembered talking to readers.

And now you were here.

So I pointed at the woman, pointed at the status window, and pointed at the empty space where the next chapter was waiting.

“All right,” I said. “Let us see what kind of world this is.”

The woman opened her mouth.

I edited the next line before she could speak.

“Welcome to another world,” she said.

Better.

The woman stared at me.

“Did you just change what I said?”

“No.”

“You did.”

“I improved it.”

Then the summoning began.
