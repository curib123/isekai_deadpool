# Chapter 005 - The Summoning Error: The Quiet Before

The locals did not meet us.

We met a ditch.

More specifically, we met a ditch, a broken road marker, and the quiet understanding that the forest had decided to keep its first impression of us unpleasant.

The woman stood on the road with her light blade raised. I stood beside her with my red sword ready. The maintenance creature perched on my shoulder and watched the trees like a tiny security guard who had been promoted beyond its emotional qualifications.

Nothing attacked.

The forest remained still.

The smoke from the valley drifted between the branches.

“You said something screamed,” I told the woman.

“Something did.”

“Then where is it?”

“It may be watching us.”

“That is rude.”

“It may be deciding whether we are worth attacking.”

“That is worse.”

“Why?”

“Because now I have to be interesting.”

The woman lowered her blade by an inch.

“You could simply remain quiet.”

I turned to the reader.

“She keeps suggesting that as if it has worked before.”

“I can hear you,” the woman said.

“I know.”

“Then stop.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you asked politely.”

She closed her eyes.

The forest made a soft clicking sound.

The maintenance creature answered.

The woman opened her eyes again.

“That is the corridor warden.”

“It has a name?”

“It is a species.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It does not need a name.”

I looked at the creature on my shoulder.

“Would you like a name?”

It clicked.

“Excellent. Your name is Bitey.”

The woman turned sharply. “No.”

“You cannot veto a name I just gave it.”

“It is not yours to name.”

“It is sitting on my shoulder.”

“It belongs to the maintenance corridor.”

“It crossed dimensions with me. That creates a relationship.”

The creature clicked again.

“Bitey agrees.”

“It cannot agree,” the woman said.

“You are being very negative about our friendship.”

The road remained empty.

We walked.

The quiet followed us.

That was the uncomfortable part. Loud danger was easy. Loud danger announced itself, provided an entrance, and occasionally came with music if the story had enough budget. Quiet danger made you wonder whether you had already missed the important moment.

I disliked quiet danger.

I also disliked that I was beginning to notice the difference.

The path widened as it descended. The black trees gave way to tall grass and clusters of purple flowers. The flowers turned toward me as I passed.

“Those are looking at me,” I said.

“They are turning toward the light.”

“The light is behind me.”

“Then they are turning toward something else.”

“You are very committed to denying the obvious.”

“You are very committed to making ordinary things strange.”

“Ordinary things have not earned my trust.”

The woman gave me a sideways look.

“You just arrived.”

“Exactly. They have had no time to prove themselves.”

We reached a flat stretch of road beside a stream. The water ran clear over white stones. On the far side stood a small wooden shelter with a roof, a bench, and a sign painted in three languages.

The first two languages were unfamiliar.

The third said **TRAVELERS MAY REST HERE. MONSTERS MAY NOT.**

I pointed at the sign.

“How does it know?”

“It is enchanted.”

“That answer is doing too much work.”

“Most answers in this world will.”

“That is a terrible system.”

“It has worked for centuries.”

“So has mold.”

The woman entered the shelter. I followed. Bitey climbed down from my shoulder and curled beneath the bench.

“It sleeps?” I asked.

“All creatures sleep.”

“I have met people who would disagree.”

“You are one of them.”

“I am recently deceased. My schedule is flexible.”

The woman stood beside the open side of the shelter and watched the road.

I sat on the bench.

It creaked.

“This bench is judging my weight.”

“You do not weigh enough to matter.”

“That was unexpectedly cruel.”

“It was a measurement.”

“You need better social instincts.”

“You need any social instincts.”

“I had some.”

“Where are they?”

I looked at my hands.

The question had teeth.

I did not answer immediately.

There were memories behind the question. A kitchen. A voice. Someone laughing at a table. A name that started with a sound I could almost reach. The image broke apart before I could touch it.

I made a joke because the alternative had sharp edges.

“I probably left them on Earth.”

The woman did not smile.

“Your memories are damaged.”

“You mentioned that.”

“You keep acting as if it is unimportant.”

“I am acting as if it is survivable.”

That silenced her.

I looked at the rain falling beyond the shelter. It drew lines through the air and broke against the grass.

“What is your name?” I asked.

“It is not relevant.”

“You know mine.”

“You told me a fictional character's name.”

“That is still more information than you have given me.”

She looked at me.

“You cannot use a false name and demand a true one.”

“I am not using a false name.”

“You are not Deadpool.”

The sentence sat between us.

I could have erased it.

I could have changed the dialogue so she said something else. I could have replaced her expression with admiration, confusion, or a sudden need to discuss the weather.

I did not.

Some sentences deserved to survive.

“You do not know that,” I said.

“I know the registry.”

“The registry does not know me.”

“That does not make you him.”

“It makes the conversation complicated.”

“It makes the conversation dishonest.”

I smiled without feeling it.

“You met me three minutes ago.”

“I have watched your behavior since the chamber.”

“Then you know I am charming.”

“I know you are avoiding fear.”

The rain became louder.

You may expect me to make a joke there.

I considered several. One involved the bench. One involved the woman. One involved an imaginary award for emotional accuracy.

None of them were good enough.

So I said nothing.

Bitey crawled from beneath the bench and placed its head against my boot.

“Even the monster is worried,” the woman said.

“Bitey is emotionally sophisticated.”

The creature clicked softly.

“See?”

“It may be hungry.”

“That is also emotional sophistication.”

The woman looked toward the road.

“We leave when the rain weakens.”

“How long?”

“Until the clouds pass.”

“Can you ask them to hurry?”

“No.”

“Can you rewrite the weather?”

I could.

The thought came easily. The rain could stop. The clouds could open. The road could dry. I could create a warm carriage, a full meal, and a road lined with polite signs explaining every danger before we reached it.

I could solve the trip before it began.

I did not.

“I could,” I said.

The woman looked at me.

“Then why not?”

I watched a drop of rain slide down the edge of the roof.

“Because I want to know what happens if I walk there.”

“You are treating this world like a game.”

“No.”

I looked at the reader.

“Games have rules.”

The woman almost smiled.

Almost.

Then the forest behind us went silent.

Not quiet.

Silent.

Every bird stopped. Every insect stopped. Even the stream seemed to lower its voice.

The woman rose.

“We move.”

“I thought we were waiting for the rain.”

“The rain is no longer the problem.”

Bitey bared all its teeth.

I stood and lifted my sword.

The road toward the valley was open.

The forest behind us was not.

Between the trees, something large shifted its weight.

The woman reached for my arm.

“When we run, do not stop.”

“I have a question.”

“Ask while running.”

“Are you going to tell me your name?”

She looked toward the forest.

“If we survive the night.”

I grinned.

“Finally. A mystery with a deadline.”

The first branch broke.

We ran toward the valley.

Behind us, the quiet ended.
