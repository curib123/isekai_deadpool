# Chapter 003 - Red Jackal Tells You: The Summoning Error: What He Notices

The summoning chamber lost its lights.

Which was unfortunate, because the open maintenance door had already provided us with a hallway full of darkness, distant breathing, and the sort of red glow that usually means somebody has made an avoidable mistake.

“I can still see,” the woman said.

“Congratulations.”

“My eyes are adapted to this chamber.”

“Then your eyes have better workplace benefits than mine.”

I could see too. Not because my eyes were special. Not because death had upgraded me. I could simply decide that the darkness was less interesting than the woman’s face, and reality politely adjusted itself.

The passage beyond the gray door became visible.

It was narrow, made of black stone, and lined with pipes that carried pale blue light instead of water. The light moved inside them like slow lightning trapped behind glass. There were doors on both sides. Most had labels. None of the labels were written in a language I knew.

One of them was written in red ink.

**DO NOT LET THE UNDEFINED ENTITY ENTER THIS CORRIDOR.**

I pointed at it.

“That sign seems relevant.”

The woman grabbed my sleeve.

“Do not touch anything.”

“I am not touching the sign.”

“You are touching the door.”

“The door is touching me back.”

“That is not how doors work.”

“You have not met the doors I know.”

The breathing came again.

Closer this time.

The woman pulled me behind her.

I looked at her back. She was tall enough to block part of my view, which was impressive because the corridor was narrow and I had not yet decided whether height worked normally in this place.

“Are you protecting me?” I asked.

“I am protecting the chamber.”

“That was not my question.”

“You are not the priority.”

“That hurt.”

“Good.”

“It did not, but I respect the effort.”

The red light at the end of the passage pulsed.

Something moved behind it.

The woman lifted one hand. Symbols appeared around her fingers. They were cleaner than the symbols on her robe. These formed a circle, then a second circle, then a third circle that looked like it had been added by a nervous assistant.

“Stand behind me,” she said.

“I am behind you.”

“Further.”

“There is a wall.”

“Then stand behind the wall.”

“The wall is behind me.”

“You are exhausting.”

“I have been told that by people who later missed me.”

The thing at the end of the corridor stepped into the light.

It was small.

That was the first thing I noticed.

The breathing suggested something large enough to eat a horse, a carriage, and the person who had sold the horse. Instead, the creature was about the size of a house cat.

It had four legs, a long tail, two curved horns, and six red eyes arranged across its face with the confidence of an artist who had never received criticism.

It also wore a tiny metal badge.

The badge read **MAINTENANCE**.

I lowered my sword.

The woman did not.

“That is a corridor warden,” she whispered.

“It is adorable.”

The creature opened its mouth.

Its jaw unfolded sideways.

Rows of teeth filled the space where a normal animal might have stored a reasonable tongue.

“It is less adorable now,” I admitted.

The creature hissed.

“Can it understand us?” I asked.

“It understands every language used in the summoning system.”

“Does it understand sarcasm?”

“No.”

“Then it has already suffered enough.”

The woman moved her hand. A spear of white light formed above the corridor.

The creature crouched.

I stepped forward.

“Wait.”

“Why?”

“Because it has a badge.”

“It is a monster.”

“It has a job.”

“It is here to remove intruders.”

“That is a job.”

“It will attack us.”

“Then we should discuss workplace safety.”

The creature launched itself.

The woman fired the light spear.

I imagined the spear becoming a harmless ribbon.

It became a harmless ribbon.

The ribbon wrapped around the creature's horns.

The creature crashed into the floor and slid between our feet.

The woman stared at the ribbon.

“You changed my spell.”

“I improved the presentation.”

“You removed its force.”

“Force is very aggressive.”

The creature rose, shook its head, and looked at me with all six eyes.

“You have offended it,” the woman said.

“I have not even spoken to it.”

“You turned its attack into a ribbon.”

“That was a visual critique.”

The creature made a clicking sound.

I crouched.

“Listen, little maintenance employee. I understand you are having a bad day. I also died recently, so I am willing to call us even.”

The creature sniffed my hand.

The woman made a sharp sound.

“Do not touch it.”

“I am not touching it.”

The creature licked my fingers.

“It is touching me.”

“That is worse.”

“Your workplace has very confusing rules.”

The creature's badge flickered.

The letters changed.

**MAINTENANCE - ERROR.**

Then:

**ENTITY RECOGNITION FAILED.**

I looked at the woman.

“Even the hallway cannot identify me.”

“The corridor is not part of the System.”

“Then your problems are spreading.”

The creature stepped aside.

The red light at the end of the passage brightened.

Beyond it, I could see another chamber. This one had a circular platform, tall crystal pillars, and a golden door standing open to a sky full of gray clouds.

Rain moved beyond the doorway.

Real rain.

I could smell wet stone and cold air.

The mortal world.

At least, that was what the woman wanted me to believe.

“The gold door is the correct path,” she said.

“You said that before.”

“And you opened the wrong one.”

“The wrong one had better lighting.”

“The wrong one contains a corridor warden.”

“A corridor warden with excellent manners.”

The creature bit the edge of my coat.

“It is eating your clothing,” the woman said.

“That is not excellent manners.”

I imagined the creature releasing my coat.

It released my coat.

I imagined it becoming a harmless stuffed animal.

It became a harmless stuffed animal.

The woman stared at it.

The stuffed animal stared back with six embroidered eyes.

“You cannot keep doing that,” she said.

“I can keep doing it.”

“You should not.”

“That is the same argument from before.”

“Because you keep being the same problem.”

“Consistency is a virtue.”

The corridor began to shake.

Dust fell from the ceiling. The blue light inside the pipes flashed faster. Somewhere behind the maintenance passage, something heavy struck a door.

The woman grabbed my wrist.

“We are leaving.”

“Now you want to go through the gold door?”

“Yes.”

“You should have started with that.”

“You opened the wrong door.”

“I was gathering information.”

“You were playing.”

The words landed harder than they should have.

I looked at her hand around my wrist.

Then I looked at the stuffed maintenance creature sitting beside my boot.

She was right.

I had been playing.

I could have closed the door. I could have erased the corridor. I could have rewritten the entire conversation so the warning sign had never existed.

Instead, I had opened it because the obvious choice looked boring.

That was the first thing I noticed about myself in the new world.

I was not afraid of power.

I was afraid of being bored with it.

“Fine,” I said. “Gold door.”

The woman released my wrist.

“You are going to follow instructions?”

“Do not get used to it.”

We stepped through the passage.

The creature followed us.

“It is coming too,” I said.

“It cannot leave the maintenance corridor.”

The stuffed animal crossed the threshold.

The woman stopped.

The creature's six eyes blinked.

I looked at you.

“That seems like a problem for the next person in charge.”

The golden door waited ahead.

Rain fell beyond it.

Wind rolled through the chamber.

The woman raised her hand toward the door.

The world outside changed.

The rain became silver.

The clouds folded into a spiral.

Far away, something enormous roared.

I gripped my red sword.

“Please tell me that was normal.”

The woman looked at me.

“No.”

“Excellent.”

The golden door opened wider.

The stuffed maintenance creature made a pleased clicking sound.

I stepped toward the storm, the woman beside me, and the wrong door's employee following at our heels.

Then the gold light swallowed us.
